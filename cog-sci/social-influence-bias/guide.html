Why Upvotes Beget Upvotes

You post a comment. Someone secretly gifts it one upvote before anyone else sees it. Five months later, it's beating its identical twins by 25%. This is that experiment — and its strange, lopsided sequel.

01 — The Hunch

Popular because it's good — or good because it's popular?

A comment sitting at +40 feels like a safer upvote than the same comment at 0. Obviously. Except you can't prove that by watching a live feed, because popular things are popular partly because they're good. Quality and prior votes arrive tangled together.

That tangle is a confound. When a highly-rated post keeps rising, is the crowd copying the crowd — or is the post just that good? Watching can't tell you. Only one thing can: randomly plant a vote on otherwise-identical content, and see what the seed alone does.

Correlation ≠ causation. To measure the indigo arrow, you have to cut the teal one — randomize the vote, hold quality constant.
The trick

Plant a secret seed vote at random. Quality now averages out across groups, so any gap in the final score was caused by that one vote. This single move unlocks every study ahead.

02 — The Clean Experiment

One secret vote, five months of consequences

In 2013, Muchnik, Aral & Taylor got to gently rig a large social-news site with up/down voting (Science). For five months, every new comment got a secret coin flip the instant it was posted: +1, −1, or nothing — before a single real reader arrived.

Randomness is the whole trick. On average, the three groups' comments were identical — same quality, same everything. So whatever gap opened up in the final scores had exactly one possible cause: that planted vote. This is the microscope Section 01 demanded.

Three arms, one coin. Quality averages out — so whatever gap appears in the final scores, the seed vote did it.

So what does one fake upvote buy? It made the first real viewer more likely to upvote too — by +32% (P = 1.0×10⁻⁶), off a small baseline of about 5.13%. Roughly 5.1% → 6.8%. Sounds modest. Except it didn't fade — it accumulated.

The seed's advantage compounds instead of washing out — modest in absolute terms, statistically unmistakable.
03 — The Twist

The crowd un-downvotes you

Here's where the story should be symmetric — and isn't. If upvotes snowball up, planted downvotes should snowball down. Instead, down-treated comments were upvoted more than control, as if the crowd smelled an unfair penalty and moved to correct it. Upvote probability: 0.099, versus 0.054 in control (P = 1.0×10⁻³⁰).

The correction all but erased the nudge: down-treated final scores ended up statistically indistinguishable from control, and not one topic category showed significant negative herding. Some herding did happen — later downvoting ticked up, 0.014 vs 0.007 — but the correction won. Praise compounds. Unfair criticism gets rebuffed.

The asymmetry: one nudge climbs unopposed; the other summons an amber counter-force that drags it back to baseline.
Why it matters

Social influence is not a symmetric force. A crowd will amplify approval but police what looks like an undeserved put-down. (Hold that loosely — Section 06 has a platform where downvotes did herd downward.)

04 — Independent Proof

MusicLab: the same song, eight different fates

Rewind seven years. Salganik, Dodds & Watts built a music market from scratch (Science, 2006): 14,341 people, 48 songs by bands nobody had heard of. One group browsed with no social information at all; eight parallel "worlds" saw one extra number — each song's running download count.

That one number did two things. Winners won bigger — success got more unequal, with the Gini coefficient higher in all eight social worlds than in the independent one (P < 0.01). And success got more unpredictable: the very same song could be a hit in one world and a flop in the next.

Show people the counts and the winners get bigger — and harder to predict. Same songs, different history, different hit.

Turn the signal up — a popularity-sorted list instead of a random grid — and both effects got stronger. Yet quality still fenced the extremes: the best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible. Success was quality × luck × cumulative advantage.

05 — Can Fake Become Real?

Tell a lie about popularity. Watch it come true.

Then Salganik & Watts did something wonderfully devious (Social Psychology Quarterly, 2008). For 12,207 new participants, they inverted the displayed download counts — the least-popular songs suddenly wore the crown. Would the crowd sniff out the lie, or make it true?

Mostly, they made it true. A song's true pre-inversion rank predicted its projected final rank at r = 0.84 in an unchanged world — but at just r = 0.16 in the inverted worlds. The fake ranking had taken over; perceived popularity became real popularity.

Fake popularity mostly comes true — but quality is a floor the best songs climb back to. (Long-run values are model projections; the experiment ended before full convergence.)

But the lie had limits. The genuinely best songs recovered despite being penalized hardest, and the whole market paid for the distortion: the appeal-to-outcome correlation fell from 0.82 to about 0.40–0.45, and people simply downloaded less (2,197 / 2,160 vs 2,898). One hedge: the long-run figures are projections — the experiment ended before the dust settled.

06 — Mechanism & Meaning

It steers your eyes, not your taste

So what is the crowd actually doing to you? Krumme and colleagues went back into the MusicLab data (PLoS ONE, 2012) and split every choice into a two-step process: first you sample a song (click to listen), then you decide whether to download it. The popularity signal touched step one only.

Counts changed which songs people sampled — but not the verdict afterward. The probability of downloading, given a listen, was statistically independent of the click. So the pull was informational (steering attention), not normative (conforming to be liked), at least in this market. And the download count's weight grew over time relative to a song's appeal — the engine of rich-get-richer.

A two-step funnel: popularity fattens the front — who clicks. What happens after the listen stays a quality call.

One tension to hold: Muchnik's comment study did find genuine opinion change, so "attention-only" may be domain-specific. That is the honest edge of this literature — convergent, but not uniform.

It replicates

Van de Rijt et al. (PNAS 2014) handed out arbitrary early success on Kickstarter, Epinions, Wikipedia and change.org — and later success followed everywhere. Lee, Hosanagar & Tan (2015) found herding in movie ratings, with the anonymous crowd outweighing friends. Cicognani et al. reproduced the positive bias by manipulating hotel ratings.

But not everywhere

The asymmetry may be platform-specific: Glenski & Weninger (2017) planted random downvotes on Reddit and got negative herding. Effects are relative changes over small absolute baselines, and they vary by topic. Van de Rijt's self-correcting dynamics show genuinely superior options tend to recover.

Mind the era

The core studies span 2006–2013 — before modern algorithmic ranking, hidden vote counts and recommender feedback loops, any of which could push cumulative advantage past what a raw displayed count does. And in most observational data, position and popularity are tangled; these RCTs are the clean exceptions.

So what?

That score under a comment is partly a social artifact, not a pure quality reading. Early votes punch far above their weight. And platform design — hiding counts, delaying their display, sorting by quality rather than raw popularity — measurably changes which content rises. Your feed is a set of choices someone made.
