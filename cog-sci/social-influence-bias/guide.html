Why Upvotes Beget Upvotes

A single planted vote can snowball into a measurably higher score. But the effect is lopsided, and quality still sets the outer limits. Here is what the experiments actually show.

Scroll to explore
01 — The Hunch

Do we upvote what's already upvoted?

It feels obvious that a comment sitting at +40 gets more thumbs-up than the same comment at 0. But you can't prove it by watching a live site, because popular things are popular partly because they're good. Quality and prior votes are tangled together.

That tangle is a confound. If a highly-rated post keeps rising, is it the crowd copying the crowd — or is the post simply excellent? Pure observation can't separate the two. To isolate social influence you have to randomly plant a vote on otherwise-identical content and watch what the seed alone does.

Correlation ≠ causation: to measure the indigo arrow, randomize the vote and hold quality constant.
The setup

Randomly assign a secret seed vote to identical content. Any difference in the final score is then caused by that one vote — not by quality. That is the causal key to every study ahead.

02 — The Clean Experiment

The study that proved the snowball

In 2013, Muchnik, Aral & Taylor ran a five-month randomized field experiment on a large social-news site with up/down voting (Science). As each comment was posted, a coin flip secretly gave it a +1, a −1, or nothing at all — before any real reader arrived.

Because the seed was random, the content was, on average, identical across groups. So whatever happened to the final score was caused by that single planted vote. This is the causal microscope the confound in Section 01 demanded.

The headline: a single planted upvote made the first real viewer +32% more likely to upvote too (P = 1.0×10⁻⁶), lifting first-viewer upvoting from a small baseline of about 5.13% to roughly 6.8%. And it didn't fade — it accumulated.

Accumulating herding: the seed's advantage grows rather than washing out — modest in absolute terms, robust statistically.
03 — The Twist

Praise compounds — unfair criticism gets rebuffed

If upvotes snowball up, you'd expect downvotes to snowball down. They don't. The down-treated comments were upvoted more than control, as if the crowd rushed in to correct an unfair penalty. Upvote probability rose to 0.099 versus 0.054 in control (P = 1.0×10⁻³⁰).

The correction so nearly cancelled the nudge that down-treated final scores ended up statistically indistinguishable from control — there was no significant negative herding in any topic category. A small herding response did occur (later downvoting ticked up to 0.014 vs 0.007), but correction dominated.

The asymmetry: positive influence accumulates; a fake penalty triggers an amber correction that erases it.
Why it matters

Social influence is not a symmetric force. A crowd will amplify approval but police what looks like an undeserved put-down. (A caveat waits in Section 06: on other platforms, downvotes have herded downward.)

04 — Independent Proof

MusicLab: one song, eight different fates

Seven years earlier, Salganik, Dodds & Watts built an artificial music market (Science, 2006). 14,341 people listened to and downloaded from 48 unknown songs. One group browsed with no social information; eight parallel "worlds" saw each song's running download count.

Showing the counts did two things. It made success more unequal — the Gini coefficient was higher in all eight social worlds than in the independent one (P < 0.01), so winners won bigger. And it made success more unpredictable: the very same song could be a hit in one world and a flop in another.

Making popularity visible amplifies inequality and scatters outcomes — success becomes path-dependent.

Making the signal more prominent — a popularity-sorted list instead of a random grid — amplified both effects further. Yet quality still fenced the extremes: the best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible. Success was quality × luck × cumulative advantage.

05 — Can Fake Become Real?

Invert the counts — and watch most of the lie stick

Salganik & Watts pushed further (Social Psychology Quarterly, 2008). With 12,207 new participants, they inverted the displayed download counts — telling people the least-popular songs were the most popular. Would the deception collapse, or become self-fulfilling?

Mostly it stuck. A song's fake starting rank predicted its projected final rank at r = 0.84 in an unchanged world, but the inversion still governed the inverted worlds at r = 0.16 against true appeal — perceived popularity manufactured real popularity.

Perceived popularity is partly self-fulfilling — but quality is a floor the best content can climb back to. (Long-run values are model projections; the experiment ended before full convergence.)

But the deception was not self-fulfilling for the market as a whole. The genuinely best songs recovered despite being penalized hardest, and the distortion degraded overall quality-matching: the appeal-to-outcome correlation fell from 0.82 to about 0.40–0.45, and total downloads dropped (2,197 / 2,160 vs 2,898). Note the long-run figures are model projections — the experiment ended before full convergence.

06 — Mechanism & Meaning

Steered attention, not peer pressure

What is social influence actually doing? Krumme and colleagues re-analysed MusicLab (PLoS ONE, 2012) and found behaviour is a two-step process: first you sample a song (click to listen), then you decide whether to download it. The popularity signal acted on step one only.

Counts changed which songs people sampled, but not their verdict after listening — the probability of downloading given a listen was statistically independent of the click. So the pull was informational (attention-steering), not normative (conforming to be liked), at least in this market. And the weight of the download count grew over time relative to a song's appeal — the engine of cumulative advantage.

A two-step funnel: popularity widens who samples a song, but the post-listen verdict stays quality-driven.

One tension to hold: Muchnik's comment study did find genuine opinion change, so an "attention-only" mechanism may be domain-specific. That is the honest edge of this literature — convergent, but not uniform.

Convergent evidence

Van de Rijt et al. (PNAS 2014) found arbitrarily bestowed early success boosted later success across Kickstarter, Epinions, Wikipedia and change.org. Lee, Hosanagar & Tan (2015) saw herding in movie ratings, with the crowd outweighing friends. Cicognani et al. reproduced the positive bias by manipulating hotel-review scores.

Boundary conditions

The asymmetry may be platform-specific: Glenski & Weninger (2017) found negative herding from random downvotes on Reddit. Effects are relative over small absolute baselines and vary by topic. Van de Rijt's self-correcting dynamics show superior options tend to recover.

Scope & caveats

The core studies span 2006–2013 — before modern algorithmic ranking, hidden vote counts and recommender feedback loops, which could amplify cumulative advantage beyond raw displayed counts. Position effects are confounded with popularity in most observational settings; these RCTs are the clean exceptions.

So what?

Vote counts and review scores are partly self-fulfilling social artifacts, not pure quality signals. Early votes matter disproportionately. And platform design — hiding counts, delaying their display, sorting by quality rather than raw popularity — measurably changes which content rises.
