SECTION I 2

The Confound

You suspect people upvote whatever's already upvoted. Try proving it by watching. You can't.
I
I · POPULAR ≠ GOOD? 3

You Can't Prove It by Watching

That comment sitting at +50 — is it genuinely good (teal)? Or did each voter just copy the votes already on the screen (indigo)?

In the wild, the two arrows arrive tangled. Good things get upvoted; upvoted things look good. No amount of watching splits them apart — correlation is not causation.

The Fix

Randomly plant a vote on otherwise-identical content. Whatever gap opens up later, only the seed could have caused it.

Ref: Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013, Science 341:647

I · THE CAUSAL KEY 4

Plant the Vote, Then Wait

Take thousands of fresh comments and flip a coin over each one: some get a secret seed vote, some don't. On average, content, timing, and authors all wash out.

So if the two groups' final scores differ months later, only one thing can explain it: the seed. That isn't a correlation. That's a cause.

Which is why the studies worth trusting here are randomized field experiments — not surveys of whatever's already popular.

Design Logic

Coin flip → identical groups → the nudge is the only difference left → whatever diverges, the nudge did it.

Ref: Muchnik et al. 2013 · van de Rijt et al. 2014, PNAS

SECTION II 5

The Experiment

Five months, a Reddit-like news site, 101,281 comments — each one silently seeded at birth. Nobody noticed.
II
MUCHNIK ET AL. 2013 · SCIENCE 6

101,281 Comments, One Coin Flip Each

For five months, every comment posted to an undisclosed social-news site was secretly routed into one of three arms the moment it was born.

Up-treated (n = 4,049): a secret +1 at creation. Down-treated (n = 1,942): a secret −1. Everyone else: control.

Then real life happened to them — >10 million views and 308,515 ratings from users who had no idea a vote had been planted.

Why It Works

One coin flip per comment. Whatever gap shows up in the final scores, the seed vote caused it — quality can't have.

Ref: Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013, Science 341:647–651

MUCHNIK ET AL. 2013 · SCIENCE 7

The First Viewer Takes the Bait

One planted upvote, and the very first person to see the comment became more likely to upvote it too. By +32% P = 1.0e-6.

The baseline is tiny — first viewers upvote about 5.13% of the time — so the nudge lifts that to roughly 6.8%.

Small per person. But it fires on the first click, before quality has had a chance to say a word.

Read It Carefully

+32% is relative, riding on a small base rate. The absolute bump (~1.7 points) is the spark — compounding does the rest.

Ref: Muchnik et al. 2013, Science 341:647

MUCHNIK ET AL. 2013 · SCIENCE 8

It Doesn't Fade — It Snowballs

You'd expect 308,515 real ratings to wash out one fake vote. They didn't. The nudge accumulated: up-treated comments ended the study with mean scores +25% higher P = 2.3e-11.

They were also +30% more likely to reach a score of ≥10 — 6.4% of treated cleared that bar, against 4.9% of controls.

Keep the scale honest: mean scores moved from about 1.9 to 2.4, and the effect clustered in certain topics.

Path Dependence

A comment nudged upward keeps attracting the votes that push it further up. Early luck compounds.

Ref: Muchnik et al. 2013, Science 341:647

SECTION III 9

The Asymmetry

Here's the twist: fake downvotes didn't work. The crowd showed up to undo them.
III
MUCHNIK ET AL. 2013 · SCIENCE 10

The Crowd Un-Downvotes You

A secret downvote should mirror the upvote and drag comments down. It didn't. Viewers upvoted the penalized comments more: probability 0.099 vs 0.054 in control P = 1.0e-30.

The crowd acted as a correction force (amber) — spotting what looked like an unfair hit and moving to undo it.

Net result: down-treated final scores were statistically indistinguishable from control. No significant negative herding, in any topic category.

The Surprise

Praise compounds. Unfair criticism gets bodyguards.

Ref: Muchnik et al. 2013, Science 341:647

MUCHNIK ET AL. 2013 · SCIENCE 11

Two Reflexes Fired at Once

Not everyone came to the rescue. A secret downvote did make later viewers a bit more likely to pile on: downvote probability 0.014 vs 0.007 in control.

So two reflexes fired at once — a small pile-on and a much bigger rescue. The rescue won.

That tug-of-war is what leaves the net downward effect statistically invisible, even though the herding impulse is real.

Nuance

Asymmetry isn't the absence of negative herding — it's correction outmuscling it on this platform.

Ref: Muchnik et al. 2013, Science 341:647

SECTION IV 12

MusicLab

Same 48 songs, nine parallel worlds — and a different hit parade in every one of them.
IV
MUSICLAB · SALGANIK 2006 · SCIENCE 13

Same Songs, Nine Parallel Earths

14,341 people wandered into a music site stocked with 48 songs by real bands nobody had heard of.

One independent world showed no social information at all. Eight social-influence worlds showed download counts — and each was free to drift its own way.

The worlds started identical, so whatever pulls them apart is seeing the counts — Muchnik's logic, scaled up to whole markets.

Setup

Same songs, same starting line. The only variable is whether — and how loudly — popularity was shown.

Ref: Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006, Science 311:854

MUSICLAB · SALGANIK 2006 · SCIENCE 14

Winners Got Bigger — and Harder to Predict

Inequality rose. Every one of the 8 social worlds ended with a higher Gini coefficient than the independent world P < 0.01 — winners won bigger, losers sank harder.

Predictability collapsed. The same song was a smash in one world and a flop in another; the worlds disagreed far more than the independent baseline allowed.

Turn the signal up — a popularity-sorted list instead of a scattered grid — and both effects got stronger.

Cumulative Advantage

Show the counts, and early leads feed on themselves: success breeds success, no extra merit required.

Ref: Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006, Science 311:854

MUSICLAB · SALGANIK 2006 · SCIENCE 15

Quality Still Guards the Extremes

Merit isn't dead, though. MusicLab's own verdict: "the best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well — but any other result was possible."

Quality draws an envelope (teal band): the very top and very bottom stay pinned. Everything between them is a wide, luck-governed middle.

In that middle, success is quality × luck × cumulative advantage — a path taken, not a verdict on merit.

The Synthesis

Herding rules the vast middle. It rarely crowns garbage or buries brilliance — but almost everything else is up for grabs.

Ref: Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006, Science 311:854

SECTION V 16

The Inversion

What if you lie about what's popular? Mostly it becomes true — but the best still fight back.
V
SALGANIK & WATTS 2008 · SPQ 17

Lie About the Charts, Watch It Come True

Round two: 12,207 new participants — and one act of sabotage. Researchers completely inverted the displayed counts, presenting the least-popular songs as the most popular.

The lie mostly stuck. In an unchanged world, early rank predicted projected final rank at r = 0.84. In the inverted worlds? r = 0.16. The fake order took over.

Popularity that people merely believed in went ahead and made itself true.

Self-Fulfilling

Tell the crowd a dud is a hit, and — for most songs — the crowd obliges.

Ref: Salganik & Watts 2008, Social Psychology Quarterly 71(4):338

SALGANIK & WATTS 2008 · SPQ 18

But the Best Songs Climb Back

A few songs refused the script. The genuinely best ones — penalized hardest by the flip — climbed back anyway.

The market still paid for the lie: the appeal→outcome rank correlation fell from 0.82 to ~0.40/0.45, and total downloads sagged (2,197 / 2,160 vs 2,898).

So a lie about popularity is self-fulfilling song by song — but not for the market as a whole. Quality claws its way back at the top.

Hedge

The long-run ranks are projections — the experiment ended before the dust settled. Direction is clear; exact endpoints are estimates.

Ref: Salganik & Watts 2008, SPQ 71(4):338

SECTION VI 19

The Mechanism

So what is the count actually doing to you? Steering your attention — your taste never gets asked.
VI
KRUMME ET AL. 2012 · PLOS ONE 20

The Count Picks What You Hear, Not What You Like

Krumme and colleagues split every MusicLab choice into two steps: first you click to listen (sample), then you decide whether to download.

Social influence acted on step 1 only. Popularity counts changed which songs people sampled — but P(download | listened) was statistically independent of the count.

Once the song was actually in someone's ears, the count lost its vote. It steered attention, not judgment.

Informational, Not Normative

The pull is a shortcut for "what's worth my time," not pressure to conform — at least in this market.

Ref: Krumme, Cebrian, Pickard & Pentland 2012, PLoS ONE 7(5):e33785

KRUMME ET AL. 2012 · PLOS ONE 21

The Count's Grip Tightens Over Time

As each world aged, the download count gained weight — mattering more and more, relative to a song's own appeal, in predicting the next click.

That is the engine of cumulative advantage (purple): early leads get louder over time. The rich get richer.

One loose thread: Muchnik's commenters did genuinely change their opinions, so the "attention-only" account may be domain-specific — normative influence can dominate elsewhere.

Caveat on Mechanism

Attention-steering explains MusicLab; it need not be the whole story on opinion-driven platforms.

Ref: Krumme et al. 2012 · cf. Muchnik et al. 2013

SECTION VII 22

Caveats & Meaning

Where the story holds, where it frays — and what it means for every score you trust.
VII
VII · CONVERGENT EVIDENCE 23

It Keeps Happening, Everywhere They Look

van de Rijt et al. 2014 (PNAS) handed out arbitrary early success on Kickstarter, Epinions, Wikipedia, and change.org — and watched it snowball on all four.

Lee, Hosanagar & Tan 2015 (Management Science) found herding in online movie ratings — with the crowd's signal outweighing friends'.

Cicognani et al. planted prior ratings on an Italian hotel in a field experiment and got the same positive social-influence bias.

Robustness

Positive social-influence bias replicates across products, platforms, and rating systems — it isn't a quirk of one site.

Refs: van de Rijt 2014, PNAS 111:6934 · Lee et al. 2015 · Cicognani et al.

VII · BOUNDARY CONDITIONS 24

Where the Clean Story Frays

The rescue reflex may be a local custom. Glenski & Weninger (2017) found negative herding from random downvotes on Reddit — no bodyguards showed up there.

And mind the scale: these are relative effects on small absolute baselines, uneven across topics. The headline percentages ride on modest raw rates.

The core studies are 2006–2013 — before modern algorithmic ranking, hidden vote counts, and recommender feedback loops, which could amplify cumulative advantage well beyond a displayed number.

Read With Care

The RCTs cleanly separate popularity from quality; most real platforms still confound position with popularity.

Ref: Glenski & Weninger 2017, arXiv:1606.06140

VII · SO WHAT 25

What That Number Actually Measures

The score under a post is not a purity meter. It's merit blended with momentum — partly a record of quality, partly a record of who happened to vote first.

Early votes punch far above their weight. A comment nudged up in its first minutes can end months ahead; the same content could have landed almost anywhere in the wide middle.

Design changes outcomes. Hide counts, delay their display, sort by quality instead of raw popularity — each measurably alters what rises.

The Thesis

Upvotes beget upvotes — asymmetrically, and bounded by quality. Popularity is real, but it is partly manufactured by being shown.

Synthesis across Muchnik 2013, Salganik 2006/2008, Krumme 2012

CONCLUSION 26

Popularity, Partly Manufactured

One secret vote, tracked for five months, turned a hunch into a measured causal fact: seeing upvotes makes people upvote more — and the nudge snowballs into a permanently higher score.

MusicLab's parallel worlds and its inversion showed the same force running whole markets — bigger winners, wilder outcomes, and false popularity that mostly makes itself true — while quality still guarded the extremes.

The Core Idea

The scores we lean on are part merit, part momentum. Early votes echo; praise compounds while unfair criticism gets rebuffed; and the crowded middle is governed as much by luck and timing as by quality.

Muchnik 2013 · Salganik 2006 & 2008 · Krumme 2012 · van de Rijt 2014 · Lee 2015 · Glenski 2017
