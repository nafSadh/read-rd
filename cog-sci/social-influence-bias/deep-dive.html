Why Upvotes Beget Upvotes
Herding, correction, and the limits of the crowd
01

The Confound: Popular or Good?

A hunch, familiar to anyone who has scrolled a comment thread: we upvote what already carries upvotes. The trouble is that this hunch is almost impossible to prove by watching. Popular things are popular partly because they are good — and good things attract votes on their own merits. When a highly-scored comment sits at the top of a thread, its score reflects both its intrinsic quality and whatever social momentum it has already gathered. Observation alone cannot separate the two.

This is the classic confound. Quality and prior popularity both push a score upward, and in ordinary data they arrive tangled together. To isolate pure social influence — the causal effect of merely seeing existing votes — one has to break the tie experimentally: randomly plant a vote on otherwise identical content and watch whether that single seed changes where the content ends up.

The Core Problem

Correlation is not causation. A score that rises with visible votes could mean people herd — or simply that good content earns votes and gets seen. Only random assignment of the very first vote can tell the two stories apart.

02

The Clean Experiment

In 2013, Lev Muchnik, Sinan Aral, and Sean Taylor ran the study that broke the confound open.[1]Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013, Science → Over five months on an undisclosed Reddit-like social-news site with up and down voting, every comment was, at the moment it was posted, randomly assigned to one of three arms: a secret +1 upvote at creation, a secret −1 downvote, or an untouched control. Users never knew a vote had been planted.

Because the seed was assigned at random, the arms started out equivalent on average. Any later divergence in a comment’s final score therefore cannot be attributed to quality, topic, timing, or author — those balance out across the groups. It can only be attributed to that one planted vote and how the crowd responded to it. That is the causal key the observational studies lacked.

The design converts a philosophical dispute into an arithmetic one. If seeing a vote does nothing, the three curves stay together. If it does something, they separate — and the amount of separation measures the pure force of social influence.

03

The Snowball: Upvotes Beget Upvotes

The curves separated, and they separated upward. A single planted upvote raised the probability that the very first person to see the comment also upvoted it by 32% (P = 1.0×10−6) — lifting a small baseline first-viewer upvote rate of about 5.13% to roughly 6.8%.[1]Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013, Science → One invisible nudge, one measurable bump in the next person’s behavior.

Crucially, the effect did not wash out as more people arrived. It accumulated. Over the five months, up-treated comments ended with mean ratings 25% higher than control (P = 2.3×10−11), and were 30% more likely to reach a score of at least ten (6.4% vs 4.9%). Each early upvote made the next one slightly more likely, which made the next one more likely still — a snowball of cumulative advantage rolling out of a single seed.

The proportions matter as much as the percentages. In absolute terms the shift is modest — mean scores moved from roughly 1.9 to 2.4, and the effect was concentrated in certain topic categories rather than uniform across the site. Social influence here is real and directional, but it is a thumb on the scale, not a landslide.

04

The Asymmetry: Praise Compounds, Criticism Rebounds

The most surprising result was what happened when the seed was negative. If herding were symmetric, a planted downvote should snowball downward the way a planted upvote snowballs up. It did not. Down-treated comments were upvoted more than control, as if the crowd rushed in to correct an unfair penalty: the probability that a viewer upvoted a down-treated comment was 0.099 versus 0.054 in control (P = 1.0×10−30).[1]Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013, Science →

The net effect: down-treated final scores came out statistically indistinguishable from control. The negative nudge was neutralized. Across every topic category, there was no significant negative herding. A subtler wrinkle survives — a downvote did slightly raise later downvoting too (0.014 vs 0.007), so a herding response and a correction response both fired — but correction dominated. Praise compounds; unfair criticism gets rebuffed.

The Surprise

Fake enthusiasm sticks and grows. Fake disapproval provokes a rescue. The vote system is not a neutral amplifier — it leans toward the positive, at least on this platform.

05

An Independent Proof: MusicLab

Seven years earlier, Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds, and Duncan Watts had reached a compatible conclusion from a different angle.[1]Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006, Science → They built an artificial music market where 14,341 participants listened to and downloaded from 48 unknown songs by real but obscure bands. Newcomers were randomly dropped into one of nine parallel universes: one independent world showing no social information, and eight social-influence worlds that displayed each song’s running download count. Same songs, same starting point — the only difference was whether you could see what others had chosen.

Showing the counts made outcomes both more unequal and more unpredictable. Inequality (measured by the Gini coefficient) was higher in all eight social worlds than in the independent world (P < 0.01): winners won bigger. And the identity of the winner became a lottery — the same song could be a hit in one social world and a flop in another, with between-world variation far exceeding the independent baseline. Making the popularity signal more salient (a sorted list rather than a randomized grid) amplified both effects.

Quality did not vanish; it set the boundaries. As the authors put it, “the best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible.” Success is neither pure merit nor pure luck — it is quality multiplied by luck and then amplified by cumulative advantage. Merit governs the extremes; social influence governs the vast, path-dependent middle.

06

Can Fake Popularity Become Real?

If a false signal is enough to launch a snowball, could an entirely fabricated popularity ranking become self-fulfilling? Salganik and Watts tested exactly this by running the MusicLab paradigm again with 12,207 participants and then completely inverting the displayed download counts — telling people the least-popular songs were the most popular.[1]Salganik & Watts 2008, Social Psychology Quarterly →

Mostly, the lie came true. A song’s pre-inversion rank predicted its projected final rank at r = 0.84 in an unmanipulated world but only r = 0.16 in the inverted worlds: perceived-but-false popularity largely became real popularity. But the market as a whole was not fully fooled — the genuinely best songs, though penalized hardest, tended to recover. The distortion degraded the market: the correlation between a song’s appeal and its outcome fell from about 0.82 to roughly 0.40–0.45, and total downloads dropped (2,197 and 2,160 in the inverted worlds versus 2,898). These long-run figures are model projections; the experiment ended before full convergence.

The Mechanism: Attention, Not Peer Pressure

What is the lever social influence actually pulls? A reanalysis of MusicLab by Krumme and colleagues found that listening-and-downloading is a two-step process: first you sample a song by clicking to listen, then you decide whether to download it.[2]Krumme et al. 2012, PLoS ONE → The popularity signal acted on step one only: it changed which songs people sampled, but the probability of downloading a song after listening to it was statistically independent of its displayed count. The pull was informational — steering attention — not normative conformity to be liked. Over time the weight of the download count grew relative to a song’s appeal, compounding the rich-get-richer dynamic.

One tension is worth flagging. Muchnik’s comment study found genuine opinion change, not merely redirected attention, so the “attention-only” mechanism may be domain-specific: informational pull in a music market, something closer to opinion contagion in a comment thread. The studies agree that social signals move outcomes; they differ on precisely how.

07

Caveats, Replications, and What It Means

The core finding — that a displayed vote count causally shapes later votes — has held up across settings and methods, but its shape depends on the platform.

Convergent Evidence

Field experiments beyond the two flagship studies point the same way. van de Rijt and colleagues found that arbitrarily bestowed early success boosted later success across Kickstarter, Epinions, Wikipedia, and change.org.[1]van de Rijt et al. 2014, PNAS → Lee, Hosanagar, and Tan documented herding in online movie ratings, finding the anonymous crowd’s influence outweighed that of friends.[2]Lee, Hosanagar & Tan 2015, Management Science → A separate field experiment manipulating prior ratings for an Italian hotel found the same positive social-influence bias.

Boundary Conditions

The asymmetry may be platform-specific. Glenski and Weninger reported negative herding from random downvotes on Reddit — the opposite of Muchnik’s no-negative-herding result — suggesting the correction reflex depends on community norms and interface.[3]Glenski & Weninger 2017 → Meanwhile, van de Rijt’s work on self-correcting dynamics shows genuinely superior options tend to recover, echoing MusicLab’s quality envelope. The effects throughout are relative shifts over small absolute baselines and are concentrated in particular topic categories.

Scope & Vintage

The core studies span 2006–2013 — before modern algorithmic ranking, hidden vote counts, and recommender feedback loops became standard. Those systems could amplify cumulative advantage well beyond the raw displayed counts these experiments manipulated. Position effects are also confounded with popularity in most observational settings; the randomized experiments above are the clean exceptions, not the rule.

So What

Review scores and vote counts are, in part, self-fulfilling social artifacts rather than pure quality signals. Early votes matter disproportionately, because they seed a path the crowd then follows. And platform design is not neutral: hiding counts, delaying their display, or sorting by estimated quality rather than raw popularity measurably changes which content rises. The crowd is wise at the extremes and suggestible in the middle — and the middle is where most content lives.

The Bottom Line

Seeing votes changes votes. The effect is causal, positive-leaning, and bounded by quality — a thumb on the scale that decides the vast middle by making it path-dependent. What looks like the verdict of the crowd is partly an echo of its own first whisper.
