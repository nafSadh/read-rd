Why Upvotes Beget Upvotes
Herding, correction, and the limits of the crowd
01

The Confound: Popular or Good?

Post a comment. Now imagine someone secretly gifts it a single upvote before anyone else lays eyes on it. Five months later, that comment is beating its identical twins by 25%. That is a real, measured result — and the interesting question is how anyone could possibly know it. Because the everyday hunch behind it — we upvote what already has upvotes — slips through your fingers the moment you try to prove it by watching. Popular things are popular partly because they are good, and good things earn votes on their own merits. A high-scoring comment at the top of a thread is two stories tangled into one number.

This is the classic confound. Quality pushes a score up. Prior popularity pushes the same score up. In ordinary data the two always arrive together, and no amount of staring separates them. To isolate pure social influence — the causal kick of merely seeing existing votes — someone has to break the tie by force: randomly plant a vote on otherwise-identical content, then watch whether that single seed changes where the content ends up.

The Core Problem

Correlation is not causation. A score that climbs alongside visible votes could mean people herd — or simply that good content earns votes and gets seen. Exactly one thing can tell those stories apart: assigning the very first vote at random.

02

101,281 Comments, One Secret Vote

In 2013, Lev Muchnik, Sinan Aral, and Sean Taylor did exactly that.[1]Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013, Science → For five months, on an undisclosed Reddit-like news site with up and down voting, every new comment was dealt one of three fates at the instant it was posted: a secret +1 upvote, a secret −1 downvote, or nothing at all — the untouched control. No user ever knew a vote had been planted.

Random assignment is the whole trick. Because chance dealt the seed, the three arms started out identical on average — same mix of quality, topics, timing, authors; it all balances out. So when a comment’s final score drifts apart from its peers’, none of those things can be the reason. Only one thing can: that single planted vote, and what the crowd did after seeing it. This is the causal key every observational study lacked.

The design turns a philosophical dispute into arithmetic. If seeing a vote does nothing, the three curves stay glued together. If it does something, they split — and the size of the split is the pure force of social influence, finally measured.

03

The Snowball: Upvotes Beget Upvotes

The curves split, and they split upward. That one planted upvote made the very first person to see the comment also more likely to upvote it — by 32% (P = 1.0×10−6), lifting a small baseline first-viewer upvote rate of about 5.13% to roughly 6.8%.[1]Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013, Science → One invisible nudge. One measurable bump in the very next human’s behavior.

And it did not wash out as the crowd arrived. It compounded. Five months on, up-treated comments carried mean ratings 25% higher than control (P = 2.3×10−11) and were 30% more likely to reach a score of at least ten (6.4% vs 4.9%). Each early upvote made the next one slightly more likely, which made the one after that more likely still — a snowball of cumulative advantage, rolled from a single flake.

Keep the absolute scale in view, though. Mean scores moved from roughly 1.9 to 2.4, and the lift was concentrated in certain topic categories rather than blanketing the site. Social influence here is real, directional, and causal — but it is a thumb on the scale, not a landslide.

04

The Asymmetry: The Crowd Un-Downvotes You

Now the surprise. If herding were symmetric, a planted downvote should snowball downward exactly the way a planted upvote snowballs up. It refused. Down-treated comments were upvoted more than control — the crowd rushed in to undo what looked like an unfair hit. The probability that a viewer upvoted a down-treated comment: 0.099, versus 0.054 in control (P = 1.0×10−30).[1]Muchnik, Aral & Taylor 2013, Science →

The net effect: down-treated final scores came out statistically indistinguishable from control. The sabotage simply evaporated. No topic category showed significant negative herding — not one. A subtler wrinkle survives: the downvote did make later downvotes slightly more likely too (0.014 vs 0.007), so a herding reflex and a correction reflex both fired — correction just won. Praise compounds. Unfair criticism gets rebuffed.

The Surprise

Fake enthusiasm sticks and grows. Fake disapproval triggers a rescue. The vote system is not a neutral amplifier — it leans positive, at least on this platform.

05

MusicLab: Nine Parallel Worlds, Same 48 Songs

Seven years earlier, Matthew Salganik, Peter Dodds, and Duncan Watts had arrived at compatible territory by a stranger route: they built parallel universes.[1]Salganik, Dodds & Watts 2006, Science → Their artificial music market let 14,341 participants listen to and download from 48 unknown songs by real but obscure bands. Each newcomer was randomly dropped into one of nine copies of the market: one independent world showing no social information, and eight social-influence worlds displaying each song’s running download count. Same songs. Same starting line. The only difference was whether you could see what everyone before you had chosen.

Seeing the counts warped the outcome in two directions at once. Success got more unequal: the Gini coefficient was higher in all eight social worlds than in the independent world (P < 0.01) — winners won bigger. And success got more unpredictable: the same song could be a hit in one social world and a flop in the next, with between-world variation far beyond the independent baseline. Turn the signal up — a popularity-sorted list instead of a randomized grid — and both distortions grew.

Quality did not vanish; it drew the boundaries. In the authors’ words, “the best songs rarely did poorly, and the worst rarely did well, but any other result was possible.” Success is neither pure merit nor pure luck — it is quality, multiplied by luck, amplified by cumulative advantage. Merit rules the extremes. Social influence rules the vast, path-dependent middle — which is where nearly everything lives.

06

Can Fake Popularity Become Real?

If one fake vote can launch a snowball, what happens if you fake the entire leaderboard? Salganik and Watts tried it. They reran the MusicLab paradigm with 12,207 participants and then completely inverted the displayed download counts — telling people the least-popular songs were the most popular.[1]Salganik & Watts 2008, Social Psychology Quarterly →

Mostly, the lie came true. In an unmanipulated world, a song’s pre-inversion rank predicted its projected final rank at r = 0.84. In the inverted worlds: r = 0.16. False popularity, once believed, largely became real popularity. But the market as a whole was not fully fooled — the genuinely best songs, though penalized hardest, tended to claw their way back. And the deception carried a cost. The correlation between a song’s appeal and its outcome fell from about 0.82 to roughly 0.40–0.45, and total downloads sagged: 2,197 and 2,160 in the inverted worlds, against 2,898. One hedge: the long-run figures are model projections — the experiment ended before the dust settled.

The Mechanism: Attention, Not Peer Pressure

So which lever is social influence actually pulling? Krumme and colleagues reanalyzed MusicLab and found the answer hiding in a two-step process: first you sample a song by clicking to listen, then you decide whether to download it.[2]Krumme et al. 2012, PLoS ONE → The popularity signal moved step one only. It changed which songs people sampled — but the probability of downloading a song after hearing it was statistically independent of its displayed count. The pull was informational — the count buys attention — not normative conformity to be liked. And the count’s weight grew over time relative to a song’s appeal, compounding the rich-get-richer dynamic.

One tension deserves flagging. Muchnik’s comment study found genuine opinion change, not merely redirected attention — so the “attention-only” mechanism may be domain-specific: informational pull in a music market, something closer to opinion contagion in a comment thread. The studies agree that social signals move outcomes; they disagree about the exact gears.

07

Caveats, Replications, and What It Means

The core finding — a displayed vote count causally shapes the votes that follow — has held up across settings and methods. Its exact shape, though, bends with the platform.

Convergent Evidence

Field experiments beyond the two flagships point the same way. van de Rijt and colleagues bestowed arbitrary early success on randomly chosen targets and watched it compound across Kickstarter, Epinions, Wikipedia, and change.org.[1]van de Rijt et al. 2014, PNAS → Lee, Hosanagar, and Tan documented herding in online movie ratings — and found the anonymous crowd swayed raters more than their own friends did.[2]Lee, Hosanagar & Tan 2015, Management Science → A separate field experiment that manipulated prior ratings for an Italian hotel landed on the same positive social-influence bias.

Boundary Conditions

The asymmetry, in particular, may not travel. Glenski and Weninger ran random downvotes on Reddit and reported negative herding — the opposite of Muchnik’s no-negative-herding result — a hint that the correction reflex depends on community norms and interface.[3]Glenski & Weninger 2017 → Meanwhile, van de Rijt’s work on self-correcting dynamics shows genuinely superior options tend to recover, echoing MusicLab’s quality envelope. And throughout, the effects are relative shifts over small absolute baselines, concentrated in particular topic categories.

Scope & Vintage

The core studies span 2006–2013 — before algorithmic ranking, hidden vote counts, and recommender feedback loops became the default. Those systems could amplify cumulative advantage well beyond the raw displayed counts these experiments manipulated. And in most observational settings, position effects are tangled up with popularity; the randomized experiments above are the clean exceptions, not the rule.

So What

The star ratings you trust and the vote counts you scan are, in part, self-fulfilling social artifacts — not pure quality signals. Early votes matter out of all proportion, because they seed the path the crowd then walks. And platform design is never neutral: hiding counts, delaying their display, or sorting by estimated quality rather than raw popularity measurably changes which content rises. The crowd is wise at the extremes and suggestible in the middle. Most content lives in the middle.

The Bottom Line

Seeing votes changes votes. The effect is causal, positive-leaning, and bounded by quality — a thumb on the scale that decides the vast middle by making it path-dependent. What looks like the verdict of the crowd is partly an echo of its own first whisper.
